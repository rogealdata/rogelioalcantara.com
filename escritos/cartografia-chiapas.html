Chiapas: Mapping the Criminal Complex

Rogelio Alcántara — Lantia Intelligence

For most of the last two decades, Chiapas sat at the margins of Mexico's organized-crime map — present, but peripheral to the conflicts reshaping the north and the Pacific coast. That has changed. The state's mix of porous borders, migratory corridors, and contested local governance has made it a live front in the expansion of the Jalisco New Generation Cartel (CJNG), and a useful case for understanding how criminal actors take territory that was never fully theirs to begin with.

The work here treats that expansion as a mapping problem before it is a narrative one: which actors hold which corridors, where control is exclusive and where it is merely contested, and how local authority — municipal, ejidal, informal — bends around a criminal presence it can rarely name in public. The method borrows from intelligence practice more than journalism: layered, source-triangulated, and revised as the map itself moves.

Territorial control in Chiapas is rarely total. It is negotiated, contested, and constantly re-drawn — which is exactly what makes it hard to see from a distance, and dangerous to describe carelessly from up close.

What the tracking shows so far is a pattern familiar from other fronts of Mexico's criminal complex, arriving late to Chiapas: an initial phase of quiet penetration through logistics and local alliances, followed by open contestation once a rival or the state pushes back. The implications extend past security policy into questions of local governance, migration management, and the everyday calculus of communities living inside a corridor they did not choose.
